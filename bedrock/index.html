Start here

There’s a belief you never question

Some things you say about yourself like they’re facts. I’m not creative. I can’t slow down. People leave. They just fall out of your mouth, and you’ve said them for so long you don’t even hear them anymore.

You’d never think to argue with any of them. They just feel true.

That’s a core belief: a story you’ve told for so long it stopped feeling like a story.

A belief you’ve never questioned feels like fact. That’s the bedrock everything else gets built on.

These beliefs guide your life. When you believe you’re capable, you take a swing at hard things. When you believe you’re not enough, you pass on the same chances and call it being realistic.

The same goes for people. If you believe everyone eventually leaves, you keep one foot out the door of every relationship you have.

Most of this guiding happens where you can’t see it, which is exactly why finding these beliefs and writing them down is worth the trouble.

Where this sits

Beliefs run upstream of your Habits, the H in the C.H.E.A.T. Code. They decide which habits feel available to you, and that decides how your Energy, Attention, and Time get spent.

Step one

Get them out of your head

Write down every story you tell about yourself, about other people, and about how the world works. Don’t sort them or fix the wording, just get them on the page.

If you go blank, that’s normal. Tap a starter to begin a sentence, or look at what you say on repeat: the advice you give everyone, the complaint that comes out of you every week, the line about yourself you deliver flat, like a fact.

Go one layer down

Some of what you wrote is surface complaints, habits and symptoms.

Use Go deeper on any line and this page will ask: if that’s true, what does it say about you?

Keep answering until you run out of answers.

That last line is bedrock, and it contains data.

Steps two and three

Where they show up. Then sort them.

Take each belief and find it in your actual behavior. What does it make you do? What does it stop you from doing? If you can’t point to something real, keep looking.

Then sort: Personal beliefs are about you. Interpersonal beliefs are about other people. Existential beliefs are about how the world works.

Step four

What it costs, what it provides

Now weigh each one.
What has it gotten you? What has it cost you?

Even the worst of these did a job for you once.

Maybe it kept you safe in a house where being useful was how you got loved.

Maybe it carried you through a workplace that punished rest.

So be honest about both sides. What it hands you, and what it takes.

Example

I have to be available. Benefits: a reputation for being reliable, two promotions on the back of it. Costs: six years of late evenings. My wife stopped asking if I was coming to dinner.

Step five

What it’s protecting

A belief that costs you this much is still here for a reason.

It was protecting something. Maybe it kept you employed. Maybe it kept you from finding out something about yourself you didn’t want to know.

Two questions for each one. What was it protecting you from back then, and if you put it down tomorrow, what are you afraid would happen?

The second one is the live question. That fear is what holds the belief in place now, and it’s what your test at the end has to go up against.

Example

I have to be available. Protected me from: being seen as replaceable in a place that cut people every spring. Afraid would happen: they’d find out things run fine without me.

/  Good place to stop  /

That’s a full sitting.

You’ve written them down, sorted them, weighed them, and named what they protect. That’s a full sitting. Stop here if you can. The next part asks whether these beliefs are even yours, and it goes better after a break. Your work is saved in this browser.

Step six

Are they even yours?

Go back through your list one more time and ask two questions of each belief. Who taught me this, and who was it good for?

You’ve heard these

Rest is something you earn.
If I slow down I’ll be replaced.
Asking for help is weakness.
If I save enough, nothing can reach me.

Nobody sat down and chose those. They came from a boss or a parent or a culture that needed something from you, and they came in so stealthy they feel like a personality now.

Example

I have to be available. Who taught it? A manager who rewarded the all-nighters, and a family where being useful was how you got loved. Who was it good for? Them. Mark it: handed to me.

      Next steps

      Live by the ones that serve you. That might mean changing a behavior, or letting go of a belief that finished its job years ago.

      Catch them in the moment. Beliefs are easiest to spot right when they fire, in the meeting or in the email you keep rewriting.

      Tell someone. Saying one of these out loud to a person you trust does something writing can’t.

      If one of them has to go

      A belief only fades when another one starts collecting proof.

      Take one you marked handed to me. Write what you’d rather carry instead, in your own words. Keep it close enough to believe today. Half-believable beats inspiring.

      Then test it once this month. Act as if the new one is true and watch what happens.
